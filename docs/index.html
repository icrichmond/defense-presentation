Chapter 1: Land-use history causes differences in park nighttime cooling capacity and forest structure

 

  • cities are temporally dynamics
  • the redevelopment of urban spaces is a social process

We asked:

  1. What are the effects of historical land-use on current park forest structure, diversity, and consequently the capacity to provide cooling?
  2. How do surrounding communities differ around parks of each historical land-use type, and what are the implications for equitable access to cooling?

We uncovered the history of parks around Montréal

We sampled temperature, forest structure, and population demographics in and around 27 parks

Past land-use affects park cooling capacity

 

  • Parks cool more during the day
  • Daytime cooling was similar across historical land-use types
  • Previously industrial sites cool less at night

Past land-use affects forest structure

 

 

  • Previously forested & agricultural sites had the highest mean basal area
  • Previously industrial sites had the largest mean tree size
  • Species richness was approximately the same

Forest structure affects cooling

 

 

  • Relationships between basal area and cooling are dependent on time of day and tree size
  • We see little effect of tree size or species richness on cooling for large trees

Put parks everywhere! … with intentional, community led approaches

  • Historical data provides useful ecological context

  • Parks cool, regardless of historical land-use

  • To improve environmental equity, green space development must be community led and paired with stronger housing regulations

Temperature regulation is variable in Montréal

 

  • Green alleys are cooler during the day than at night
  • In Montreal, green alleys can be warmer or cooler than grey alleys

In Trois-Rivieres, green alleys cool at night

 

  • Green alleys are providing important benefits

  • Different management approaches result in different benefits

    • Montréal is more variable and also more flexible
    • TR is less variable but less able to adapt to resident needs

  • Right now, green alleys are providing more biodiversity support than ecosystem services with respect to benefits provided by trees

  • We recommend a bottom-up approach paired with expert support and ongoing funding to maximize the potential of green alleys

Chapter 3: Predictors of land-surface temperature and air pollution across Canadian cities are largely scale and city dependent

Chapter 3: Predictors of land-surface temperature and air pollution across Canadian cities are largely scale and city dependent

Cities are complex and novel ecosystems

Cities are complex and novel ecosystems

Adapted from Pickett et al. 2017, Urban Ecosystems

We asked

  1. What is the relative contribution of street trees to predicting urban heat and air pollution at different scales when compared to urban form and sociodemographics?

  2. What are the relationships between important predictors of temperature and air pollution in different cities and across scales?

We measured temperature, air pollution, ecological form, urban form, and sociodemographics across 7 Canadian cities

 

  • Ecosystem service indicator: land surface temperature, air pollution (CO, NO2, SO2, UV, O3)

  • Ecological form: street tree diversity/size/density, greenness

  • Urban form: building cover/height, road size/density, greyness

  • Sociodemographics: income, race/ethnicity, immigration status, housing

We measured our variables at multiple scales

  • Extracted at the most important predictors of temperature and air pollution across cities/scales
  • Assessed if there were generalizable patterns

Inequity at the city scale was one of very few general predictors

 

Ecological form was an important predictor of temperature at the city and neighbourhood scales

Predictive models can help us identify areas for further research

  • We need to assess scale-specific interventions for temperature and air-pollution related issues in cities

  • Trees are most likely affecting the environmental conditions much more than is indicated in this study

  • More multi-scale, multi-service studies are needed in Canada

 

Thesis Take-Aways

  • Integrating historical conditions facilitates understanding of current conditions

  • Spatial scale is of critical importance when discussing nature’s benefits

  • Small, unconventional green infrastructure types are deserving of our attention

  • Interdisciplinary work improves our ability to respond to the needs of people
